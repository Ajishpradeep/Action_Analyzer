# **Architecting an AI-Driven Gamification System for Eco-Friendly Civic Behavior in Taiwan**

The transition toward sustainable civic behavior relies heavily on the implementation of robust waste management systems and the active participation of the populace. Taiwan represents a globally recognized paradigm of effective waste management, having transitioned from a period of high waste generation to a highly structured circular economy. Central to this achievement is the "Four-in-One Resource Recycling Plan," initiated in 1997, which integrates community residents, recycling enterprises, municipal cleaning squads, and the government's recycling fund1. Through mandatory garbage sorting—enforced by a per-bag trash collection fee system—Taiwan achieved a waste recycling rate exceeding 60.22% by 2017, significantly reducing the daily per capita garbage clearance rate1.  
However, the current paradigm relies predominantly on punitive measures and logistical mandates. The Environmental Protection Administration enforces strict sorting protocols, and failure to appropriately separate general waste from recyclables and food waste can result in fines ranging from NT![][image1]6,0002. While negative reinforcement has successfully established a baseline of compliance, there is a strategic opportunity to deploy artificial intelligence to shift the behavioral driver toward positive reinforcement.  
The proposed initiative envisions a production-ready, highly contextualized AI module designed to score and reward eco-friendly civic behavior. By allowing users to upload images or egocentric (first-person) videos of daily sustainable actions, the system will evaluate the context, verify the action against a localized knowledge base, and dispense micro-rewards. This report provides a comprehensive architectural blueprint, detailing the requisite multimodal AI models, the construction of a Taiwan-specific localized knowledge base, anti-fraud verification mechanisms, and an exhaustive taxonomy of generalizable eco-actions that can be integrated into this behavioral reward ecosystem.

## **System Architecture: The Multimodal Retrieval-Augmented Generation (MM-RAG) Pipeline**

To process unstructured visual data—such as user-uploaded images and videos of unstructured daily activities—and accurately map them to specific municipal regulations, the system requires a decoupled architecture that separates visual perception from logical reasoning and contextual retrieval. Traditional text-only Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems are insufficient for this task, as converting complex visual scenarios into flattened text descriptions strips away spatial, temporal, and relational context, leading to hallucinations and lost insights4.  
The architecture must instead utilize a Multimodal Retrieval-Augmented Generation (MM-RAG) framework. An MM-RAG system operates by encoding disparate data types, including text, images, and video frames, into a shared mathematical embedding space, enabling cross-modal semantic matching5. The system pipeline encompasses several distinct phases of data transformation and analysis.  
The initial phase involves modality encoding. Raw data from the user's mobile device is ingested, and specialized encoders act as translators to convert each modality into an understandable vector representation. Textual metadata is processed using transformer models, audio data is processed via frameworks like wav2vec, and visual data is processed through Vision Transformers or Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) such as the Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) model4. These vectors are stored in a high-capacity vector database, such as Pinecone, which allows for low-latency, nearest-neighbor similarity searches at scale5.  
When a user submits a query—for instance, a video of themselves recycling a specific type of plastic container—the MM-RAG system converts this query into a temporary embedding. The system then queries the vector database, which houses Taiwan's specific environmental regulations and visual examples of compliance, to retrieve the relevant localized context5. To handle the high computational load and preserve logical integrity, the architecture can implement a Query Complexity Router, similar to the AutoThinkRAG framework. This router analyzes the difficulty of the user's upload and adaptively selects the most efficient reasoning path, decoupling the visual interpretation performed by a lightweight Vision-Language Model (VLM) from the logical deduction performed by a Large Language Model (LLM)6. This bifurcation significantly reduces token consumption and monetary inference costs while maximizing verification accuracy6.  
The final phase of the pipeline relies on an agentic verifier, utilizing frameworks such as Argos. Argos is designed to improve the reliability of reinforcement learning in multimodal models by rewarding the AI not just for arriving at the correct answer, but for grounding that answer in actual visual and temporal evidence7. The verifier checks two critical conditions: whether the objects and events referenced actually exist within the user's uploaded video, and whether the model's reasoning aligns with the temporal sequence of events7. If the verifier confirms that an eco-friendly action has been completed in accordance with the retrieved local regulations, an API call is triggered to a reward gateway, dispensing digital currency such as the Taiwan EPA's Green Points (環保集點).

## **Algorithmic Interpretation of Egocentric Video Data**

Because users will record their sustainable actions using handheld smartphones, the visual data is inherently "egocentric," captured from a first-person perspective. Egocentric video analysis presents unique computational challenges that differ drastically from traditional third-person surveillance video processing. The footage is characterized by severe, non-linear camera shake, unpredictable lighting conditions, and frequent occlusion of objects by the user's own hands8.  
To accurately score actions in this chaotic visual environment, the AI must rely on object-centric representations rather than traditional full-body pose estimation11. In an egocentric paradigm, activities such as washing a paper container or dropping a battery into a bin are modeled as temporally consistent interactions between the user's hands and the target objects12.  
The AI model must continuously track these interactions across frames. To compensate for the erratic camera motion inherent in handheld smartphone recording, the system utilizes optical flow modalities. Optical flow quantifies the apparent motion between consecutive images by calculating a velocity vector for each pixel9. The motion of the egocentric camera is predominantly driven by the 3D rotation of the user's body and arms, which can be mathematically compensated for by applying a 2D homography transformation to the image sequence. This transformation stabilizes the background environment, allowing the model to isolate and analyze the specific kinetic movement of the object being manipulated10.  
Furthermore, to ensure the application remains highly accurate over time, the system should implement Continuous Online Action Detection (COAD). Traditional action detection models are trained offline on static datasets, which often leads to degraded performance when deployed in the wild due to highly specific, personalized user behaviors and changing household environments13. COAD enables the model running on the user's edge device to learn on the fly from the continuous video streams it processes. By updating its weights using local data, the model adapts to the specific lighting conditions of the user's kitchen or the unique shape of their local recycling bins, improving top-5 prediction accuracy by up to 20% over static models13.

## **Waste Classification via Convolutional Neural Networks**

For static image uploads and individual video frame analysis, the system requires a highly optimized object detection backbone capable of identifying and classifying specific waste materials with industrial precision. The YOLO (You Only Look Once) family of deep learning models has demonstrated profound efficacy in this domain, offering an optimal balance between processing speed and classification accuracy, making them highly suitable for edge deployment on mobile devices14.  
The YOLO architecture is structured into three primary components: the Backbone, the Neck, and the Head. In architectures such as YOLOv4, the Backbone utilizes CSPDarknet53 to extract low-level and high-level image features. The Neck employs a Spatial Pyramid Pooling (SPP) module and a Pyramid Aggregation Network (PANet) to fuse these features across different scales, enhancing the network's ability to recognize objects of varying sizes15. Finally, the Head network applies anchor boxes on the feature map to generate an output vector containing the object's predicted class, confidence score, and bounding box coordinates15.

Empirical research validates the use of YOLO architectures for waste classification in Taiwan. Models trained on the Taiwan Recycled Waste Dataset (TRWD)—a dataset specifically tailored to the unique packaging shapes and waste characteristics found in the region—demonstrated exceptional performance. For instance, a YOLOv3 model trained on the TRWD achieved a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 92.12% at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5015.  
Subsequent iterations, such as YOLOv7, have pushed this performance even further. While consumer smartphones lack hyperspectral sensors, a YOLOv7 model fine-tuned on localized data will be more than capable of processing high-resolution RGB inputs to identify standard household waste items.  
To maximize the system's resilience to real-world clutter, the model should be trained using semi-supervised learning pipelines. By deploying multiple AI models to evaluate unlabelled footage and generate high-quality pseudo-annotations, researchers have successfully doubled the mean average precision of waste detectors compared to models trained strictly on sanitized academic datasets19. This ensures the model remains robust when faced with the messy reality of a Taiwanese household or alleyway.

## **Constructing the Localized Knowledge Base**

An AI model capable of recognizing a plastic bottle or a paper box is insufficient in isolation; the system must comprehend exactly *how* that specific item should be processed within the specific jurisdiction of the user. Taiwan's waste management ecosystem is highly localized, with varying rules across its 22 counties and cities. The MM-RAG knowledge base must ingest, index, and retrieve data across several operational vectors to ensure accurate scoring.

### **Integration with Municipal Open Data APIs**

The foundation of the localized knowledge base is built upon continuous data ingestion from the Ministry of Environment (MOENV) and municipal Environmental Protection Bureaus. Taiwan provides extensive Open Data portals, allowing developers to query live environmental statistics and logistics data via RESTful APIs20.  
The application architecture will utilize these APIs to retrieve JSON or XML formatted data, authenticating via registered API keys20. By parameterizing the API calls with specific data IDs, limits, offsets, and temporal formats (e.g., year\_month=2026\_07), the backend server can maintain an updated repository of local regulations, collection schedules, and facility locations20. This data is subsequently vectorized and embedded into the MM-RAG database.

### **Paper Container Protocol: Clean, Sort, Stack**

A persistent point of confusion among the public is the categorization of paper meal containers, such as bento boxes and coffee cups. While they appear to be paper, they are lined with a Polyethylene (PE) plastic film to prevent oil and liquid leaks. Consequently, they cannot be processed with general paper waste; they must be sent to specialized processing plants that can separate the plastic film from the paper fibers26.  
To address this, the localized knowledge base must enforce the government's "Clean, Sort, Stack" (清、分、疊) protocol28. If a user requests a reward for recycling a paper bento box, the MM-RAG verification agent will query this specific rule. The visual pipeline must confirm three sequential states from the user's video:

> 1. **Clean:** The AI must detect that the user has wiped or rinsed the container, confirming the absence of heavy oil stains or food residue26.  
> 2. **Sort:** The AI must verify that the item is placed with other 3D containers (plastics, cans) and entirely separated from general waste paper (like newspapers or cardboard boxes)26.  
> 3. **Stack:** The AI must observe the user nesting the items tightly together to reduce the physical volume before disposal26.

If the AI detects excessive oil or food remnants, it will reject the reward claim and trigger a localized educational prompt, reinforcing the behavioral correction required to improve downstream recycling efficiency.

## **Defeating Fraud: Anti-Spoofing and System Integrity**

Because the proposed system dispenses tangible financial value—in the form of Green Points, EasyCard top-ups, or shopping discounts—it will inevitably become a target for fraudulent exploitation. Malicious users may attempt to bypass the system by uploading stock photographs of recycling, replaying pre-recorded videos on a secondary screen, or utilizing physical masks and printed media to mimic compliance.

### **Presentation Attack Detection (PAD)**

In the field of biometric and action verification, these fraud attempts are classified as Presentation Attacks (PAs). A highly robust Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) framework is a mandatory component of the architecture30.  
The system cannot rely on static image matching. Geometrically, a high-definition printed photograph of a properly sorted recycling bin is mathematically equivalent to the live object when viewed through a single monocular smartphone camera31. To combat this, the AI must mandate video uploads and evaluate active liveness cues.  
The PAD framework will analyze dynamic texture and motion features within the video sequence. It will observe the kinetic physics of the scene—for instance, how ambient light dynamically reflects off a plastic bottle as it falls into a bin, or how the user's hand casts shifting shadows across the background. Static photographs, 3D printed objects, and flat electronic screens fail to accurately replicate these complex spatiotemporal micro-intensity changes30. Furthermore, the system can utilize remote PhotoPlethysmoGraphy (rPPG) to measure facial micro-intensity changes corresponding to the user's blood pulse, guaranteeing that a live human is operating the camera30.  
To augment the PAD framework, the system will utilize instruction-tuned Vision-Language Models (VLMs), such as the InstructFLIP architecture. InstructFLIP explicitly decouples the AI's training instructions into two components: *content*, which encodes the specific semantics of spoofing attacks (e.g., detecting the bezel of a replayed video screen or the matte texture of a printed photo), and *style*, which accounts for environmental noise and varying camera qualities32. This decoupling allows the model to generalize highly effective anti-spoofing measures across entirely new, unpredictable environments.

### **Mitigating Injection Attacks**

Beyond physical spoofing, sophisticated threat actors may attempt system-level "injection attacks." In this scenario, the attacker bypasses the smartphone's physical camera hardware entirely, utilizing software manipulation to inject a fake, pre-recorded, or AI-generated video directly into the application's data stream via an API hook33.  
To counteract this vulnerability, the mobile application must securely sandbox the camera API. It must refuse uploads originating from the device's camera roll or file system, mandating that all actions be recorded live within the application's secure environment34. Furthermore, the backend system must execute rigorous metadata analysis. It will extract EXIF data, GPS coordinates, and timestamp telemetry to verify that the submission aligns with expected parameters34.  
As a final layer of security, the system can implement randomized challenge-response mechanisms. During a recording, the application may prompt the user to perform a specific, unpredictable micro-action—such as rotating the battery twice before dropping it, or holding up a specific number of fingers. If the AI does not detect this specific kinematic action in the video, the submission is flagged as a likely replay attack and rejected34.

## **A Taxonomy of Rewardable Eco-Actions: Expanding the Gamification Matrix**

The foundational architecture—multimodal action recognition coupled with localized knowledge retrieval and rigorous anti-fraud measures—can be scaled horizontally to encompass a vast array of sustainable behaviors beyond household waste sorting. By integrating with existing Taiwanese corporate ESG initiatives and government platforms, the system can act as a centralized "Eco-Ledger." The following subsections outline a brainstormed catalog of actions that the general public can perform to earn rewards.

### **Battery and Electronic Waste Recycling**

Taiwan consumes approximately 11,000 tonnes of dry-cell batteries annually, yet the recycling rate hovers around 40%, meaning roughly 7,000 tonnes remain unaccounted for36. Improper disposal leads to severe environmental degradation due to the leaching of heavy metals such as iron, manganese, zinc, and cadmium36.  
The system's knowledge base will index all approved battery disposal vectors and incentivize citizens to utilize them. Major convenience store chains, such as 7-Eleven and FamilyMart, offer aggressive recycling rewards, providing an NT$11 shopping discount for every 0.5kg of used batteries brought to the counter36. Furthermore, private startups like Ecoco operate 24-hour smart recycling hubs in cities like Hsinchu and Tainan. These hubs accept D, C, AA, AAA, AAAA, and 9-volt batteries (excluding coin cells) in exchange for points37.  
The AI module will utilize the geolocation data attached to the user's video submission. If the GPS coordinates match a known 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, or Ecoco smart hub, the system will look for specific visual markers, such as the store's designated recycling bin or the automated reverse vending machine (RVM) interface38. Users successfully uploading proof of battery recycling will receive the store's inherent discount, alongside multiplier bonus Green Points from the application. The system can easily extend this model to general electronic waste, rewarding users for dropping off old laptops, mobile phones, and chargers at designated community electronic disposal hubs40.

### **Smart Recycling Booths (iTrash)**

To provide citizens with more flexibility than the scheduled garbage truck routes, municipal governments are deploying smart recycling infrastructure. The Taipei City Government successfully piloted "iTrash" stations—24-hour smart recycling booths that integrate directly with the EasyCard (悠遊卡) payment system42.  
These sophisticated IoT machines compress waste, deodorize, and utilize cloud computing to automatically notify cleaning crews when they reach 80% capacity43. Users are charged a nominal fee for general garbage (NT![][image3]1 for every 10 PET bottles or 8 aluminum cans)43. Similar systems have been deployed in Taoyuan's Daxi Old Street45.  
The proposed AI app can act as a gamification aggregation layer over this existing hardware. By syncing with the user's EasyCard data via secure API, the app can read the transaction history and provide a gamified dashboard. Users who consistently utilize iTrash stations can unlock digital badges, climb regional leaderboards, and earn multiplier bonuses, driving higher utilization rates for the municipal hardware34.

### **Food Waste Reduction: Time-Based Pricing Rescue**

Food waste is a critical environmental issue, generating massive carbon emissions during production, transport, and disposal. Taiwan's incredibly high density of convenience stores historically led to immense volumes of food waste as expired bentos and onigiri were discarded daily47.  
To combat this, retail chains have implemented sophisticated "time-based pricing" systems powered by "time-control barcodes." These barcodes automatically reduce the price of fresh food items as they approach their expiration deadlines, ensuring that expired items cannot be sold while simultaneously incentivizing the purchase of near-expired goods47.

### **The "Circular Cup" (循環杯) Ecosystem**

To curb the astronomical volume of single-use beverage containers, Taiwan's Environmental Protection Administration instituted aggressive mandates. As of July 2022, all beverage shops and convenience stores must offer a minimum NT$5 discount to consumers who provide their own cups53. By 2023, major chains were mandated to provide reusable "Circular Cups" at a minimum of 5% of their locations, scaling to 10% in 2024 and 30% by 202553.  
The AI system can deeply integrate with this initiative. Users can record themselves borrowing or returning a Circular Cup at a convenience store kiosk. The AI will utilize object detection to read the specific QR codes or visual markers on the cup, verify its return to the designated sanitation bin, and award points53. The localized knowledge base will house the specific hygiene standards required by the government, ensuring the user is not attempting to return a cup filled with non-beverage trash, which violates sanitation protocols55.

### **Dynamic Integration with the Garbage Truck Network**

Unlike many Western municipalities, Taiwanese residents do not leave bins on the curb for automated pickup. Instead, they must carry their sorted trash to designated street corners at highly specific scheduled times to meet musical garbage trucks playing *Für Elise* or *A Maiden's Prayer*44.  
The AI system will integrate directly with the open data platforms provided by the MOENV and local bureaus, utilizing the real-time GPS tracking APIs to pinpoint truck locations21. The application can reward civic altruism directly related to this logistical challenge. For example, if the AI detects a younger user assisting an elderly neighbor in carrying their waste to the truck—verified through egocentric video interaction and facial anonymization algorithms—it can dispense a high-value civic duty reward. Furthermore, by gamifying the prompt arrival at the truck, the app discourages the illegal dumping of waste on street corners by individuals who missed their scheduled pickup time.

### **Low-Carbon Mobility and the "Carbon Passbook"**

The "My Carbon Passbook" (我的減碳存摺) initiative, a joint operation by the municipal governments of Taipei, New Taipei, Keelung, and Taoyuan, rewards citizens for utilizing green public transportation60. By linking a registered EasyCard, the system calculates carbon savings based on the distance traveled via MRT, bus, or YouBike, compared to the baseline emissions of a passenger car61. Users achieving specific thresholds (e.g., 20kg of carbon reduction per month) qualify for lotteries and physical rewards60.  
The AI application will integrate seamlessly with the Carbon Passbook API, displaying the user's progress within the unified gamified dashboard62. Furthermore, the app can independently verify zero-carbon micro-mobility. For instance, the Carbon Passbook dictates that a 15kg carbon reduction is roughly equivalent to walking 200,000 steps a month63. The app can request authorization to access the device's native pedometer data (Apple Health or Google Fit). If the user achieves step goals instead of taking short car trips, the system validates the data and awards corresponding Green Points.

### **Resource Sharing and Upcycling**

Finally, the system can reward the extension of product lifecycles through donation and upcycling. Platforms like the GC Gift Network (GC贈物網) allow users to share idle household goods and clothing. The Taiwan EPA already supports this by awarding 100 Green Points to both the giver and the receiver for every successful item exchanged on the platform65. The AI app can facilitate these exchanges, using object detection to categorize the donated items and automatically processing the point transfers, thereby reducing the volume of perfectly usable goods entering the waste stream.

## **Strategic Implications and Future Scalability**

The implementation of an AI-driven, gamified reward system carries profound socio-economic implications. By assigning a tangible micro-value to daily civic duties, the system bridges the gap between macro-environmental policy and micro-economic consumer incentives.

### **The Network Effect of Financial Gamification**

Gamification systems that rely solely on virtual badges often suffer from rapid user churn once the initial novelty fades. To ensure long-term behavioral change, the system must anchor its rewards in real-world utility34. By converting earned Green Points into direct financial equivalents (where 100 points equal NT$1)66, or by directly integrating top-ups with the universally utilized EasyCard, the system eliminates the friction of abstract digital currency. Users can seamlessly apply their rewards to pay for MRT rides, purchase items at convenience stores, or offset utility bills. This financial anchoring creates a persistent network effect, driving continuous engagement.

### **Scalability Beyond the Taiwanese Paradigm**

While the knowledge base described herein is deeply localized to Taiwan's specific regulations—such as the unique three-category sorting mandate, localized bag colors, and local corporate recycling programs—the underlying architectural framework is entirely agnostic.  
To deploy this system in alternative global municipalities, engineers do not need to redesign the core AI architecture. They merely need to swap the regulatory documents within the vector database and adjust the GPS-fenced API endpoints to match the new locality4. The decoupled nature of the agentic verifier means the underlying YOLO and Vision-Language Models do not require complete retraining from scratch; they merely evaluate the visual input against the new set of localized rules retrieved by the RAG module6.

### **Optimization of Municipal Telemetry**

The data generated by widespread application usage will provide municipal governments with unprecedented, high-resolution telemetry regarding citizen behavior. By aggregating anonymized data, the Environmental Protection Administration can generate heatmaps of battery recycling frequency, receive real-time feedback on neighborhood compliance with paper container washing protocols, and track peak usage times for iTrash booths. This granular data allows authorities to optimize garbage truck routing, dynamically allocate recycling resources to high-yield neighborhoods, and target public education campaigns with absolute precision, fulfilling the mandate for AIoT-based smart environmental governance45.

## **Conclusion**

The conceptualization of an AI module to score and reward eco-friendly behavior presents a transformative opportunity to digitize, incentivize, and optimize civic sustainability. By leveraging state-of-the-art YOLO object detection frameworks and multimodal Vision-Language Models tailored for egocentric action recognition, the system can reliably interpret the complex, unpredictable reality of daily human activity.  
Crucially, pairing this advanced perceptual engine with a Multimodal RAG architecture ensures that the AI's evaluations are strictly grounded in Taiwan's highly specific local regulations—ranging from the nuances of "Clean, Sort, Stack" for paper containers to the precise logistics of convenience store battery recycling and municipal garbage truck schedules. When fortified with stringent anti-spoofing and liveness detection protocols, the architecture creates a secure, fraud-resistant ledger of environmental stewardship.  
Ultimately, by transforming routine chores into a rewarding, gamified experience integrated seamlessly with national platforms like the Green Point system and EasyCard, this technology has the potential to drastically accelerate the transition toward a complete circular economy. It provides an actionable blueprint demonstrating that the most powerful tool for systemic environmental change is positive, individualized reinforcement powered by localized artificial intelligence.
