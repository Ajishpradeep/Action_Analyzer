# Taiwan’s Waste Rules & Recycling Guidelines  
Taiwan enforces **strict waste sorting**.  Residents must buy designated, color-coded trash bags (per district) to cover disposal fees.  Waste is divided into **general waste**, **recyclables**, and **food waste**.  General (non-recyclable) trash goes in the district’s garbage bags and is picked up by one truck; recyclables (paper, plastics, metals, glass, batteries, electronics, etc.) go in another bin/bag and are collected separately.  Guidelines emphasize that recyclables be **cleaned and prepared** – e.g. rinse bottles, flatten cans, remove caps – before recycling.  **Hazardous items** like dry-cell batteries, light bulbs, and e-waste must be **stored separately** and delivered to designated collection points (e.g. community drop-offs or convenient-store kiosks).  (Mis-sorting can incur fines of NT$1,200–6,000.)  All these local rules would feed into the app’s knowledge base so it knows, for example, that *throwing a battery into a general bag* is incorrect, whereas *taking it to a convenience-store recycle bin* is correct.

# Computer Vision & Action Recognition Models  
To verify user uploads, the app can leverage **state-of-the-art CV models**.  For **images**, object detectors (e.g. YOLOv5) can identify items like plastic bottles, aluminum cans, paper, clothes, electronics or batteries.  For instance, researchers have used YOLO to recognize waste categories (plastic, glass, metal, paper, biodegradable, etc.) with high accuracy.  Similarly, a CNN/R-CNN pipeline was shown to classify e-waste from smartphone photos with ~90–97% accuracy.  For **video clips**, we can use 3D convolutional networks that capture motion.  Modern architectures like **SlowFast networks** process videos at two speeds (a “fast” branch with many frames for motion, and a “slow” branch with fewer frames for detail).  Such models (pretrained on action datasets like Kinetics400) can be fine-tuned to recognize eco-friendly actions (e.g. “placing item in recycle bin” vs. “throwing in trash”).  In practice, one could use a combination: first detect key objects (bottles, bins) with an image model, then apply an action classifier (SlowFast, I3D, or even Vision-Language models like CLIP) to confirm that the user actually **deposited** the item correctly.  

## Knowledge-Base Design (Actions → Rules → Rewards)  
Build a **structured knowledge base** mapping items/actions to local rules and reward points.  For example:  

- **Items & Destinations:** (“Plastic bottle” ⇒ “recycling bin”, “AA battery” ⇒ “convenience-store battery bin”) based on EPA guidelines.  Dry batteries and bulbs go to designated drop-offs; food scraps may go in a green bin (in cities with organics collection).  
- **Preparation Rules:** (e.g. “Rinse and flatten cans/bottles”).  The AI could check for clean vs. dirty items.  
- **Context & Location:** Tag known recycling sites (e.g. map family-mart or 7-11 branches with drop-off machines, municipal recycling centers).  If a video shows a recognizable logo or geotag of a recycle kiosk, match it against the database of valid drop-offs.  
- **Rewards Schema:** Assign points per action.  For instance, FamilyMart’s system gives **10 points** for each AA/AAA battery and **2 points** per PET plastic bottle.  (This suggests a scheme like +10 for battery, +2 for bottle, etc.)  The knowledge base would encode these values.  

Using official examples: some Taiwanese cities already issue points for battery recycling.  These can inform our point system.  For instance, Hsinchu’s “battery hubs” award GreenPoints (100 pts = NT$1) and Ecoco points per battery collected.  We might similarly tie validated actions to GreenPoints or Ecoco points.  All these mappings (item ⇒ proper disposal method ⇒ point value) form the core rules the AI checks against.  

# Scoring Logic & Verification Workflow  
- **Analysis Step:** When a user uploads media, run it through the CV pipeline.  Detect objects (bags, bottles, cans, batteries, bins, logos) and classify the action (e.g. “throwing bottle”, “holding battery near bin”).  Compare against the knowledge base rule: e.g. “Is the plastic bottle entering a recycling container?” If the model confidently sees correct sorting, flag as success.  
- **Scoring:** Award the predefined points.  For example, if the image clearly shows a recycled PET bottle in the right bin, add 2 points; if it shows a battery being dropped in a recognized recycling box, add 10 points.  (A video sequence could yield points for each item disposed.)  Points might scale with complexity (e.g. more points for large electronics vs. small bottles).  
- **Fraud Prevention:** Ensure submissions are **live and unique**.  Require users to capture photos/videos from within the app (so metadata/timestamp is logged).  The app can display a random short code on-screen that the user must show in the video (challenge–response) to prove freshness.  We can also limit frequency (e.g. max one submission per action type per day) and check for duplicate images.  Any suspicious case (low confidence or reused image) could be queued for manual review.  
- **Confirmation Workflow:** Optionally, users could first **select** the action type (e.g. “recycle plastic”, “drop battery”) in the app, so the system knows which rule to apply.  After the AI verifies the media, the app updates the user’s score and (if integrated) credits their GreenPoint account.  We should inform users if an upload fails verification and why (e.g. “Unrecognized bin” or “Item appears unsorted”).  

# Additional Eco-Actions & Reward Programs  
Beyond trash sorting and battery drop-offs, many everyday “green” behaviors could be scored:  

- **Recyclable returns:** Taking plastic bottles/aluminum cans to return machines (like those at FamilyMart).  The AI could verify this if the machine interface is visible in the shot.  
- **E-waste recycling:** Bringing old electronics or light bulbs to an official collection point (often conveniece stores or EPA centers).  A photo of handing an old phone at a recycle drop could qualify.  
- **Reusable usage:** Using cloth shopping bags or re-usable containers.  For example, a photo of a cashier scanning a reusable mug (or showing a loyalty card for refillable coffee) could prove an eco-action.  
- **Food waste recycling/composting:** Capturing one’s compost bin of kitchen scraps (after removing packaging).  Some Taiwanese cities have kitchen waste pick-up; showing a compostable bag with food scraps could count.  
- **Community actions:** Participating in neighborhood cleanups, tree-planting, or beach clean events.  A group photo (with app confirmation) or selfie at the site can earn points.  
- **Energy/conservation:** Though harder to verify by image, actions like switching to LED bulbs (showing a receipt or bulb in hand) or saving water might be gamified with creative proof (e.g. scanning a “water-saving” QR code in the app after performing an energy audit).  

To amplify rewards, the app can integrate with existing systems: Taiwan’s **GreenPoint** platform already lets users earn points for eco-actions.  We could automatically convert validated actions into GreenPoints (e.g. a completed recycling upload adds to their EPA GreenPoint balance).  Local incentives like Ecoco’s programs are relevant too: Ecoco’s “circular economy” apps let users redeem points at stores for recycling bottles and batteries.  For example, Hsinchu’s smart battery hubs give 250 GreenPoints per battery (worth NT$2.50) plus Ecoco points.  Similarly, FamilyMart’s kiosk rewards 10 points per AA battery and 2 points per PET bottle.  We would mirror those values so users feel consistent rewards.  

**In summary**, the system would use CV models (YOLO, SlowFast, etc.) to recognize correct eco-actions in user photos/videos.  A Taiwan-specific knowledge base encodes local disposal rules and reward rates.  Verified actions earn digital points, potentially integrated with Taiwan’s GreenPoint/Ecoco ecosystem, incentivizing a wide range of everyday green behaviors.
